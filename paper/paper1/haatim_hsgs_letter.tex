\begin{abstract}
Rydberg atomic receivers sense a radio field through an optical transition and
report its amplitude alone, so the phase is lost at the point of measurement.
This paper asks when it helps to constrain the channel in such a receiver. On a
uniform linear array the channel across antennas is a sum of a few sinusoids,
which is a strong constraint but a bounded one: the array size fixes how much
structure it can carry. Two properties of the receiver settle the question in
advance. Each amplitude reading constrains the channel in one direction only,
and readings at different antennas are independent of one another, so extra
antennas do not improve accuracy per antenna unless the estimator exploits how
the channel varies across them. The constraint should therefore help when the
channel is simple relative to the array's capacity and hurt otherwise, which is
what we measure: averaged over twelve operating points per array size, it gains
$+2.93$~dB at $32$ antennas and loses $0.25$~dB at $8$, while the unconstrained
baseline varies by at most $0.163$~dB. The loss appears only in summed squared
error; under a per-trial median the same cells give $+0.225$~dB, because the
estimator declines the constraint in $42\%$ of trials. The governing quantity is
not the path count but the fraction of the array's capacity the channel
occupies, and it predicted the gain on two unseen channel distributions to
within $0.13$ and $0.315$~dB. Results are simulation only, and the bounds
computed here govern unbiased estimators while every estimator studied is
biased.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{S}{ixth-generation} systems are expected to operate at higher
carrier frequencies and over wider bandwidths than their predecessors, and both
trends press on the receiver: higher frequencies mean weaker incident fields,
and wider bandwidths mean the front end must stay linear and calibrated across
more spectrum. Conventional receivers answer with metallic antennas and
electronic amplification, whose sensitivity is bounded by thermal noise in the
antenna and the first amplifier stage, and which must be recalibrated band by
band. Quantum sensing has emerged as one response, and among its candidates the
Rydberg atomic receiver has drawn the most attention for
communications~\cite{cui2025,gong2025mag}. Such a receiver replaces the antenna
with a glass cell of alkali vapour whose atoms are laser-excited into high-lying
Rydberg states. An incident radio field shifts the atomic energy levels, and a
probe laser crossing the cell carries that shift out as a change in optical
transmission, so sensitivity is set by atomic physics rather than circuit noise,
and the response is retuned by changing laser frequencies rather than by
rebuilding hardware. One consequence organises everything that follows: the
optical readout responds to the \emph{amplitude} of the radio field, so the
phase is destroyed at the point of measurement and never appears at the output.

Channel estimation is therefore not a linear inverse problem: recovering a
complex channel from amplitude-only measurements is phase retrieval, non-convex
and without a closed form, made tractable here by a known reference field that
converts relative phase into observable amplitude. Existing estimators for this
receiver improve the solver --- alternating
projection~\cite{gerchberg1972}, gradient descent with a rank
projection~\cite{xu2025}, or an unrolled network~\cite{xiao2026} --- but none
addresses when constraining the channel is worth doing at all. A constraint is
reported as helpful because it helped at the array size and channel richness
simulated, with no account of when it would stop helping or start hurting. That
gap is the subject of this paper: under what conditions does imposing structure
actually improve a magnitude-only estimator, and how could a designer tell in
advance?

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item We show that the information carried by an amplitude-only measurement is
block diagonal across receive elements under the unconstrained parameterisation
of the channel, so that normalised per-element information does not grow with
array size, and we identify the consequence: aperture is usable only by an
estimator that exploits how the channel varies across elements.
\item We derive the capacity of the exponential-sum structure on a uniform
linear array, obtain from it a prediction that a rank constraint must fail and
then do harm at small aperture, and test that prediction against measurement.
\item We replace the path count by the effective rank of the channel relative
to that capacity, and separate what the constraint contributes from what the
estimator's decision rule contributes.
\item We freeze the resulting relation, commit numerical predictions, and then
evaluate them on two channel distributions that played no part in constructing
it.
\end{itemize}

\section{System Model and Assumptions}
\label{sec:model}

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\resizebox{0.97\columnwidth}{!}{\input{fig/fig0_system.tex}}
\caption{Uplink of the atomic receiver. $K$ single-antenna users transmit
pilots $\bS$ over a geometric multipath channel to $N$ vapour cells arranged as
a uniform linear array. Each cell is interrogated by probe and coupling lasers
and illuminated by a known reference beam $\bB$. Photodetection reports the
magnitude of the total field, which is where the phase is lost, and the
estimator sees only $\bZ$.}
\label{fig:system}
\end{figure}

An $N$-element uniform linear array (ULA) of vapour cells serves $K$
single-antenna users over $P$ pilot symbols, as in Fig.~\ref{fig:system}. With
$\bG\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times K}$ the channel, $\bS$ the pilots, $\bB$ the known
reference beam and $\bW$ the noise, the receiver observes
\begin{equation}
\bZ=\left|\bG\bS+\bB+\bW\right|,\qquad \bW_{np}\sim\CN(0,\sigma^2),
\label{eq:obs}
\end{equation}
with $|\cdot|$ elementwise. The modulus is applied after the noise is added,
which is what makes \eqref{eq:obs} impossible to linearise without
approximation. Each user's channel is a sum of $L_k$ plane waves on the array
manifold,
\begin{equation}
g_k[n]=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_k}\alpha_{\ell,k}e^{-\jmath(n-1)\psi_{\ell,k}},\qquad
\psi_{\ell,k}=\pi\sin\theta_{\ell,k},
\label{eq:chan}
\end{equation}
where $\psi$ follows from half-wavelength element spacing. The
reference-to-signal ratio (RSR) is the reference power divided by the
\emph{single-user} received power, and the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is the
single-user received power divided by $\sigma^2$; both are defined at the array
and are design parameters rather than measured quantities.

\textbf{Assumptions.} The model above rests on the following, stated so that
each can be attacked separately.
\begin{itemize}
\item Sparse geometric multipath, with $L_k$ small relative to $N$.
\item Path gains $\alpha_{\ell,k}$ i.i.d.\ circularly symmetric complex
Gaussian, with equal expected power across paths.
\item Angles of arrival i.i.d.\ uniform on $(-90^\circ,90^\circ)$, with
$L_k\sim\mathcal{U}\{3,\dots,7\}$~\cite{xu2025}.
\item Narrowband and frequency flat: no delay spread, so path delays across the
array act as pure carrier phase.
\item Equal user power, so near--far effects are untested here.
\item Pilots drawn once per trial and shared bit-identically by every estimator
compared.
\item Noise added before the modulus, as in \eqref{eq:obs}.
\item Reference beam known at the receiver in both amplitude and phase.
\end{itemize}

\section{Proposed Estimator}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Structure and its capacity}

Substituting \eqref{eq:chan} into the $(N-p)\times(p+1)$ Hankel lifting
$\Hank_p$ separates the exponential in its two indices, so each path
contributes one rank-one outer product and $\rank\Hank_p(\bg_k)\le L_k$. The
lifting is therefore rank deficient whenever the channel has few
paths~\cite{cadzow1988,markovsky2008}. Because $\Hank_p$ has dimensions
$(N-p)\times(p+1)$, the largest rank it can carry is
\begin{equation}
\rmax(N)=\max_p\min(N-p,\,p+1)=\lceil N/2\rceil,
\label{eq:cap}
\end{equation}
and if $L_k\ge\rmax(N)$ the constraint says nothing. This yields a prediction
available before any simulation: at $N=8$ the capacity is $4$ while the
simulated path counts run from $3$ to $7$, so the constraint is frequently
vacuous and, when it does bite, removes genuine components. A rank constraint
should therefore help at large aperture and harm at small.

The user count cancels: the structural model uses $3\sum_k L_k$ parameters
against $2NK$ unstructured, a ratio independent of $K$, so the gain is expected
to be invariant to the number of users at fixed pilot adequacy.

\subsection{The estimator}

After every exact expectation--maximization Gerchberg--Saxton (EM-GS) update,
the proposed estimator applies a Cadzow projection
$\mathcal{P}_r(\bg)=\Hank_p^{\dagger}(\Pi_r(\Hank_p(\bg)))$, in which $\Pi_r$
truncates to the $r$ largest singular values and $\Hank_p^{\dagger}$ averages
along antidiagonals. One application is \emph{not} idempotent: it is a single
step of an alternating projection between the set of rank-$r$ matrices and the
set of Hankel matrices, so the number of sweeps is part of the operator's
definition and not a tuning detail. With the projection disabled the
estimator reproduces EM-GS to $\max|\text{diff}|=0$, so the projection is
provably the only difference between the two arms compared throughout.

\subsection{Choosing the order without an oracle}

The rank $r$ is not known and is not supplied. A fraction $\nu=0.3$ of the
pilot columns is held out, each candidate $r\in\{1,\dots,\rmax\}$ is scored by
the magnitude residual on those held-out columns, and the minimiser is used.
An in-sample criterion cannot serve: constraining $\bG$ can only worsen the fit
to the pilots used for fitting, so the in-sample residual always prefers the
largest candidate. The true $L_k$ never enters, so the rule uses no oracle.
When the selector returns $\hat r\ge\rmax$ the projection is the identity and
the estimator \emph{abstains}, falling back exactly to EM-GS. This is designed
behaviour rather than a failure mode, and it turns out to carry much of the
method's protection against a badly chosen constraint.

Subspace methods such as root-MUSIC and ESPRIT would recover
% Named without a citation on purpose: see docs/open-todos.md item 2.
$\{\psi_{\ell,k}\}$ directly, but they need the signal subspace, which requires
either multiple snapshots or the complex field. With magnitude-only
single-snapshot data that subspace is identifiable only after phase retrieval
has succeeded, so we regularise toward the exponential-sum manifold rather than
parameterising it.

Structured low-rank Hankel recovery for sums of exponentials is well developed
for \emph{linear} measurements~\cite{chen2014emac,jin2016aloha,haldar2014loraks},
as are gridless line-spectrum methods under a separation
condition~\cite{tang2013offgrid}. Neither applies here: the measurement is a
modulus, so no convex relaxation is available and the projection can act only as
a regulariser inside a non-convex iteration.

\section{Performance References}
\label{sec:crlb}

\subsection{Information in an amplitude measurement}

Each observation is Rician and depends on the noiseless field only through its
modulus, so it constrains the channel along a single direction: the information
it contributes is rank one~\cite{balan2015}, and for this observation model the
exact Rician form is derived in~\cite{cui2025}. Crediting both quadratures
% src: results/track_b/constrained_crlb.json (unconstrained_rank1), 400 trials,
% the 36 points of the N x P x SNR grid shared by configurations A and B
instead yields a reference too low by $0.17$--$4.64$~dB across our $36$
operating points, and at $K=3$, $P=3$ it reports full rank where the per-element
problem is in fact unidentifiable. The existing bound for this
receiver~\cite{xu2025} is computed from a linearised model; the one used here
keeps the exact Rician form.

The consequence matters more here than the form. Because the $n$th row of
$\bG\bS$ enters only the $n$th row of the measurement, the information matrix
is block diagonal across receive elements, so the per-element problem is
unchanged by adding elements and normalised per-element information does not
grow with $N$. An unstructured estimator therefore gains nothing from a larger
array, and aperture is available only to an estimator that couples elements ---
which is what \eqref{eq:cap} supplies.

\subsection{A reference that accounts for the structure}

The ordinary Cram\'er--Rao bound describes an estimator that treats every
channel entry as a free unknown. The proposed estimator instead uses the fact
that the channel is a sum of a few sinusoids, and the constrained bound is the
corresponding limit for estimators that do; the gap between the two is what the
structure is worth in principle. Under the geometric model the parameters number
about $45$ and, unlike the $2NK$ unstructured coordinates, that count does not
grow with $N$. The textbook constrained form is unusable because the Jacobian
% src: results/track_b/constrained_crlb.json, mean jacobian_rank over the 12
% N=8 cells = 40.380; 3*mean(sum L_k) = 3 x 14.875 = 44.66; mean(3 sum L_k >
% 2NK = 48) over 6400 stored trials = 28.1%. Verified in
% reports/HSGS_PAPER_PROVENANCE.md:205-214.
$\bD$ is rank deficient, its mean numerical rank at $N=8$ being $40.4$ against
$\approx44.7$ parameters, so we use the tangent-space
form~\cite{gorman1990,stoica1998}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CCRB}=\bU(\bU^T\bJ\bU)^{-1}\bU^T,
\label{eq:ccrb}
\end{equation}
with $\bU$ an orthonormal basis for the directions the geometric model can
reach, which is well defined when $\bD$ is not full rank.

Both references constrain the covariance of \emph{unbiased} estimators. Every
estimator here runs to a fixed iteration budget with a shrinking update, and the
proposed one additionally truncates rank, so none is unbiased and neither
reference is a bound on what we measure. We report them as reference curves,
with the constrained one primary because it is matched to the estimator's model
class, and the unconstrained one secondary because it describes the baseline
only.

\subsection{Effective rank, and the constraint versus the estimator}
\label{sec:effrank}

$\reff=\exp(-\sum_i p_i\ln p_i)$ with $p_i=\sigma_i/\sum_j\sigma_j$, normalised
as $\rho=\reff/\rmax$. A $16$-path channel on a $32$-element array is

\subsection{Prediction on unseen channel distributions}
\label{sec:crossgen}

A relation fitted and evaluated on the same data proves little. We froze the
$\rho$-to-gain relation obtained above, committed numerical predictions to
version control, and only then ran two channel generators that had played no
part in constructing it. The rule is piecewise-linear interpolation on a fixed
eight-point table, with no regression and no spline, so it re-executes exactly.
Both tests use configuration~C's operator and operating point with the
generator replaced.

% src: reports/trackD_partB9_analysis.json, B6_xiao.B6_xiao_clustered
For a clustered Saleh--Valenzuela generator at $\rho=0.331$ the relation
predicted $+1.30$~dB, and the measurement over $276$ trials gave $+1.173$~dB
with a confidence interval of $[+1.017,+1.380]$, an error of $0.13$~dB. For a
% src: reports/trackD_partB9_analysis.json, B8_cui_measured
second, independently specified configuration at $\rho=0.400$ the prediction was
$+0.648$~dB with interval $[+0.367,+1.015]$, and the measurement over $266$
trials gave $+0.963$~dB with a confidence interval of $[+0.808,+1.086]$, an
error of $0.315$~dB --- inside the stated interval but on its upper edge.

These are cross-generator and not out-of-model tests: both generators still
produce channels of the form \eqref{eq:chan}, and only the distribution over
$(L_k,\alpha_{\ell,k},\theta_{\ell,k})$ changes. They show that $\rho$ transfers
across path distributions, not that it survives a violation of the ULA model.

\label{sec:kinv}

Order selection accounts for $57.7$--$85.5\%$ of runtime, so it, and not the
projection, is where an implementation should look for savings.

% src: results/p12/B5.json, 300 trials/point, shared \hat r, configuration A
Comparing interleaved projection against a single post-hoc projection at the
same selected order, at configuration~A, interleaving is worse at $0$~dB
($-0.164$~dB, interval $[-0.317,-0.066]$), indistinguishable from $+5$ to
$+15$~dB, and better only at $+20$~dB ($+0.168$~dB, interval
$[+0.102,+0.246]$), the six-point mean being $+0.003$~dB. Interleaving is
therefore not required in this classical iteration.

Since placement is a wash, what the estimator contributes is not \emph{where}
the projection is applied but \emph{whether} it is applied at all and at what
order, decided from held-out pilots and using no oracle. The evidence is the
abstention result above: the selector is what keeps $\dhs\ge0$, and forcing the
projection past the crossing costs $-0.075$~dB with interval
$[-0.186,-0.026]$. The order selector is the claim of this paper, not a
mechanism explained on the way to one.

\section{Limitations and Conclusion}

An amplitude-only receiver's information is block diagonal across elements, so
aperture is usable only through cross-element structure; the exponential-sum
structure of a ULA channel supplies it, with capacity $\lceil N/2\rceil$. The
constraint therefore helps only below a fraction of that capacity, the predicted
sign reversal at small aperture is observed, and the governing coordinate
$\reff/\rmax$ predicted the gain on two unseen channel distributions to within
$0.13$ and $0.315$~dB.

\textbf{Limitations.} All results are simulation only, with no measured
hardware and no recorded channel data. They cover geometric ULA channels of the
form \eqref{eq:chan} at the array sizes, pilot counts and generators tested, and
the cross-generator tests change the distribution but not the model family, so
no claim is made beyond them. The gain magnitude does not collapse onto the
proposed axis and its residual of $0.493$~dB is unexplained, and $\rho$ is not
receiver-observable. Two of the three boundary crossings cannot be bracketed
from the negative side, because abstention prevents it, and $\rho\approx0.75$ is
% src: rydberg_sim/channel.py:245 raises above L=48 at N=64
unreachable at $N=64$ in any case, where the path generator fails above $L=48$
($\rho\approx0.67$). We establish no fundamental bound: the references govern
unbiased estimators and every estimator here is biased.
