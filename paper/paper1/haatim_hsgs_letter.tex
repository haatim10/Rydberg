\begin{abstract}
Receivers built from Rydberg atoms sense a radio field through an optical
transition, and the quantity they report is the field's amplitude alone; the
phase never reaches the output. Estimating a multi-user channel from such a
receiver is therefore harder than ordinary linear estimation.

We study when it helps to tell the estimator in advance that the channel cannot
be arbitrary. On a uniform linear array the channel across antennas is a sum of
a few sinusoids, one per propagation path --- a strong constraint, but a limited
one, since the array size fixes how much structure it can carry. We impose that
constraint inside an iterative amplitude-only estimator and choose how hard to
impose it from the pilots alone, using no knowledge of the true channel. Two
properties of the receiver predict the outcome beforehand: each amplitude
reading pins the channel down in one direction only, and readings at different
antennas say nothing about one another, so adding antennas does not by itself
improve accuracy per antenna. A larger array helps only an estimator that uses
how the channel varies from one antenna to the next.

The constraint helps when the channel is simple relative to what the array can
represent, and hurts otherwise. Averaged over twelve operating points at each
array size, it gains $+2.93$~dB at $32$ antennas and loses $0.25$~dB at $8$,
while the unconstrained baseline varies by at most $0.163$~dB across the same
range. The loss appears only in summed squared error; under a per-trial median
the same cells give $+0.225$~dB, because the estimator declines the constraint
in $42\%$ of trials and is then identical to the baseline. What governs the
outcome is not the number of paths but how much of the array's capacity the
channel occupies, and that quantity predicted the gain on two channel
distributions it was never fitted to, to within $0.13$ and $0.315$~dB.

Results are simulation only. The bounds computed here describe unbiased
estimators and every estimator studied is biased, so they serve as references
rather than limits.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{S}{ixth-generation} systems are expected to operate at higher
carrier frequencies and over wider bandwidths than their predecessors, and both
trends press on the receiver: higher frequencies mean weaker incident fields,
and wider bandwidths mean the front end must stay linear and calibrated across
more spectrum. Conventional receivers answer with metallic antennas and
electronic amplification, whose sensitivity is bounded by thermal noise in the
antenna and the first amplifier stage, and which must be recalibrated band by
band. Quantum sensing has emerged as one response, and among its candidates the
Rydberg atomic receiver has drawn the most attention for
communications~\cite{cui2025,gong2025mag}. Such a receiver replaces the antenna
with a glass cell of alkali vapour whose atoms are laser-excited into high-lying
Rydberg states. An incident radio field shifts the atomic energy levels, and a
probe laser crossing the cell carries that shift out as a change in optical
transmission, so sensitivity is set by atomic physics rather than circuit noise,
and the response is retuned by changing laser frequencies rather than by
rebuilding hardware. One consequence organises everything that follows: the
optical readout responds to the \emph{amplitude} of the radio field, so the
phase is destroyed at the point of measurement and never appears at the output.

Channel estimation for such a receiver is therefore not a linear inverse
problem. Recovering a complex channel from amplitude-only measurements is a
phase-retrieval problem, non-convex and without a closed-form solution, and it
is made tractable here by a known reference field added at the array, which
converts relative phase into observable amplitude. Existing estimators for this
receiver take the problem as given and improve the solver: alternating
projection in the style of Gerchberg and Saxton~\cite{gerchberg1972}, gradient
descent with a rank projection~\cite{xu2025}, or an unrolled network trained on
simulated channels~\cite{xiao2026}. What none of them addresses is when
constraining the channel is worth doing at all. A structural constraint is
reported as helpful because it helped at the array size and channel richness
that were simulated, and there is no account of the conditions under which it
would stop helping, or start hurting. That gap is the subject of this paper:
under what conditions does imposing structure on the channel actually improve a
magnitude-only estimator, and how could a designer tell in advance?

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item We show that the information carried by an amplitude-only measurement is
block diagonal across receive elements under the unconstrained parameterisation
of the channel, so that normalised per-element information does not grow with
array size, and we identify the consequence: aperture is usable only by an
estimator that exploits how the channel varies across elements.
\item We derive the capacity of the exponential-sum structure on a uniform
linear array, obtain from it a prediction that a rank constraint must fail and
then do harm at small aperture, and test that prediction against measurement.
\item We replace the path count by the effective rank of the channel relative
to that capacity, and separate what the constraint contributes from what the
estimator's decision rule contributes.
\item We freeze the resulting relation, commit numerical predictions, and then
evaluate them on two channel distributions that played no part in constructing
it.
\end{itemize}

\section{System Model and Assumptions}
\label{sec:model}

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\resizebox{0.97\columnwidth}{!}{\input{fig/fig0_system.tex}}
\caption{Uplink of the atomic receiver. $K$ single-antenna users transmit
pilots $\bS$ over a geometric multipath channel to $N$ vapour cells arranged as
a uniform linear array. Each cell is interrogated by probe and coupling lasers
and illuminated by a known reference beam $\bB$. Photodetection reports the
magnitude of the total field, which is where the phase is lost, and the
estimator sees only $\bZ$.}
\label{fig:system}
\end{figure}

An $N$-element uniform linear array (ULA) of vapour cells serves $K$
single-antenna users over $P$ pilot symbols, as in Fig.~\ref{fig:system}. With
$\bG\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times K}$ the channel, $\bS$ the pilots, $\bB$ the known
reference beam and $\bW$ the noise, the receiver observes
\begin{equation}
\bZ=\left|\bG\bS+\bB+\bW\right|,\qquad \bW_{np}\sim\CN(0,\sigma^2),
\label{eq:obs}
\end{equation}
with $|\cdot|$ elementwise. The modulus is applied after the noise is added,
which is what makes \eqref{eq:obs} impossible to linearise without
approximation. Each user's channel is a sum of $L_k$ plane waves on the array
manifold,
\begin{equation}
g_k[n]=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_k}\alpha_{\ell,k}e^{-\jmath(n-1)\psi_{\ell,k}},\qquad
\psi_{\ell,k}=\pi\sin\theta_{\ell,k},
\label{eq:chan}
\end{equation}
where $\psi$ follows from half-wavelength element spacing. The
reference-to-signal ratio (RSR) is the reference power divided by the
\emph{single-user} received power, and the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is the
single-user received power divided by $\sigma^2$; both are defined at the array
and are design parameters rather than measured quantities.

\textbf{Assumptions.} The model above rests on the following, stated so that
each can be attacked separately.
\begin{itemize}
\item Sparse geometric multipath, with $L_k$ small relative to $N$.
\item Path gains $\alpha_{\ell,k}$ i.i.d.\ circularly symmetric complex
Gaussian, with equal expected power across paths.
\item Angles of arrival i.i.d.\ uniform on $(-90^\circ,90^\circ)$, with
$L_k\sim\mathcal{U}\{3,\dots,7\}$~\cite{xu2025}.
\item Narrowband and frequency flat: no delay spread, so path delays across the
array act as pure carrier phase.
\item Equal user power, so near--far effects are untested here.
\item Pilots drawn once per trial and shared bit-identically by every estimator
compared.
\item Noise added before the modulus, as in \eqref{eq:obs}.
\item Reference beam known at the receiver in both amplitude and phase.
\end{itemize}

\section{Proposed Estimator}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Structure and its capacity}

Substituting \eqref{eq:chan} into the $(N-p)\times(p+1)$ Hankel lifting
$\Hank_p$ separates the exponential in its two indices, so each path
contributes one rank-one outer product and $\rank\Hank_p(\bg_k)\le L_k$. The
lifting is therefore rank deficient whenever the channel has few
paths~\cite{cadzow1988,markovsky2008}. Because $\Hank_p$ has dimensions
$(N-p)\times(p+1)$, the largest rank it can carry is
\begin{equation}
\rmax(N)=\max_p\min(N-p,\,p+1)=\lceil N/2\rceil,
\label{eq:cap}
\end{equation}
and if $L_k\ge\rmax(N)$ the constraint says nothing. This is the sense in which
the array size limits how much structure the model can express, and it yields a
prediction available before any simulation: at $N=8$ the capacity is $4$ while
the simulated path counts run from $3$ to $7$, so the constraint is frequently
vacuous and, when it does bite, removes genuine components of the channel. A
rank constraint should therefore help at large aperture and harm at small.

The user count cancels from this comparison. The structural model uses
$3\sum_k L_k$ parameters against $2NK$ unstructured, and the ratio does not
depend on $K$, so the gain is expected to be invariant to the number of users
at fixed pilot adequacy.

\subsection{The estimator}

After every exact expectation--maximization Gerchberg--Saxton (EM-GS) update,
the proposed estimator applies a Cadzow projection
$\mathcal{P}_r(\bg)=\Hank_p^{\dagger}(\Pi_r(\Hank_p(\bg)))$, in which $\Pi_r$
truncates to the $r$ largest singular values and $\Hank_p^{\dagger}$ averages
along antidiagonals. One application is \emph{not} idempotent: it is a single
step of an alternating projection between the set of rank-$r$ matrices and the
set of Hankel matrices, so the number of sweeps is part of the operator's
definition and not a tuning detail. We claim no new projection algorithm;
Cadzow's~\cite{cadzow1988} is used as given. With the projection disabled the
estimator reproduces EM-GS to $\max|\text{diff}|=0$, so the projection is
provably the only difference between the two arms compared throughout.

\subsection{Choosing the order without an oracle}

The rank $r$ is not known and is not supplied. A fraction $\nu=0.3$ of the
pilot columns is held out, each candidate $r\in\{1,\dots,\rmax\}$ is scored by
the magnitude residual on those held-out columns, and the minimiser is used.
An in-sample criterion cannot serve: constraining $\bG$ can only worsen the fit
to the pilots used for fitting, so the in-sample residual always prefers the
largest candidate. The true $L_k$ never enters, so the rule uses no oracle.
When the selector returns $\hat r\ge\rmax$ the projection is the identity and
the estimator \emph{abstains}, falling back exactly to EM-GS. This is designed
behaviour rather than a failure mode, and it turns out to carry much of the
method's protection against a badly chosen constraint.

Subspace methods such as root-MUSIC and ESPRIT recover $\{\psi_{\ell,k}\}$
% Named without a citation on purpose: see docs/open-todos.md item 2. The
% argument depends only on both methods needing the signal subspace, not on
% any specific result in those papers, and no verified entry exists here.
directly from the signal subspace, which would make the structure explicit
rather than imposed. That subspace, however, requires either multiple snapshots
or the complex field, and with magnitude-only single-snapshot data it becomes
identifiable only after phase retrieval has already succeeded --- at which
point the channel is estimated and there is nothing left to do. We therefore
regularise toward the exponential-sum manifold instead of parameterising it.

Structured low-rank Hankel recovery for sums of exponentials is well developed
outside this setting. EMaC~\cite{chen2014emac}, ALOHA~\cite{jin2016aloha} and
LORAKS~\cite{haldar2014loraks} recover such a matrix from \emph{partial linear}
observations by convex relaxation with recovery guarantees, and gridless
line-spectrum and atomic-norm methods~\cite{tang2013offgrid} likewise assume
linear measurements and a separation condition. Both families differ from the
present problem in the same way: the measurement here is a modulus, so the map
is not linear, no convex relaxation applies, and the projection can act only as
a regulariser inside a non-convex iteration.

\section{Performance References}
\label{sec:crlb}

\subsection{What an amplitude measurement does and does not reveal}

Conditioned on the channel, the pilots and the reference, the noiseless field at
one element and one pilot instant is a fixed complex number, and the observation
is its magnitude corrupted by complex Gaussian noise. The resulting density is
Rician, and it depends on that complex number only through its modulus. A
measurement of this kind therefore constrains the channel along a single
direction in parameter space: it says how large the field is, and nothing about
which way it points. Writing $\boldsymbol\eta$ for the real and imaginary parts
of $\bG$ stacked, the information contributed by element $n$ is a sum of $P$
rank-one terms,
\begin{equation}
\bJ_n=\sum_{p=1}^{P}4\beta(a_{n,p},\sigma^2)\,
\mathbf{u}_{n,p}\mathbf{u}_{n,p}^{T},
\label{eq:fisher}
\end{equation}
with $\beta$ a scalar depending on the field magnitude $a_{n,p}$ and the noise
power, and $\mathbf{u}_{n,p}$ the direction the measurement constrains.

\textbf{This is not new and we do not claim it.} Balan~\cite{balan2015} gives
the rank-one form in general, for any likelihood depending on a complex field
only through its modulus, and Cui \emph{et al.}~\cite{cui2025} derive the exact
Rician case for this observation model as a sum of rank-one terms. We restate
it because the consequence we need follows from it and, as far as we are aware,
has not been drawn. Crediting both quadratures instead --- treating each
measurement as if it constrained two directions --- overstates the information
% src: results/track_b/constrained_crlb.json (unconstrained_rank1), 400 trials,
% the 36 points of the N x P x SNR grid shared by configurations A and B
and yields a reference too low by $0.17$--$4.64$~dB across our $36$ operating
points. An identifiability check settles which form is right: at $K=3$, $P=3$
the per-element problem is unidentifiable and \eqref{eq:fisher} is correctly
singular, whereas the rank-two construction reports full rank. We note also
that the existing bound for this receiver~\cite{xu2025} is computed from a
linearised model, whereas \eqref{eq:fisher} keeps the exact Rician form.

\textbf{The consequence.} Because the $n$th row of $\bG\bS$ enters only the
$n$th row of the measurement, $\bJ$ is block diagonal across receive elements.
The per-element estimation problem is then unchanged by adding elements, so
normalised per-element information does not grow with $N$, and an unstructured
estimator gains nothing from a larger array. Aperture is available only to an
estimator that couples elements together --- which is what \eqref{eq:cap}
supplies.

\subsection{A reference that accounts for the structure}

The ordinary Cram\'er--Rao bound describes an estimator that treats every
channel entry as a free unknown. The proposed estimator does no such thing: it
uses the fact that the channel is a sum of a few sinusoids, and it should not be
judged against a reference that ignores that fact. The constrained bound is the
corresponding limit for estimators that do use it, and the gap between the two
is what the structure is worth in principle. Under the geometric model the
parameters $\boldsymbol\phi$ number about $45$ and, unlike the $2NK$
unstructured coordinates, that count does not grow with $N$.

The textbook constrained form is unusable here because the map from geometric

% N=8 cells = 40.380; 3*mean(sum L_k) = 3 x 14.875 = 44.66; mean(3 sum L_k >
% 2NK = 48) over 6400 stored trials = 28.1%. Verified in

parameters to channel entries has a rank-deficient Jacobian $\bD$ --- at $N=8$
its mean numerical rank is $40.4$ against $\approx44.7$ parameters. We use
instead the tangent-space form of Gorman and Hero and of Stoica and
Ng~\cite{gorman1990,stoica1998},
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CCRB}=\bU(\bU^T\bJ\bU)^{-1}\bU^T,
\label{eq:ccrb}
\end{equation}
where $\bU$ is an orthonormal basis for the directions the geometric model can
actually reach. This depends on that set of directions and not on how the model
is parameterised, which is what makes it well defined when $\bD$ is rank
deficient.

Both references constrain the covariance of \emph{unbiased} estimators. Every
estimator here runs to a fixed iteration budget with a shrinking update, and the
proposed one additionally truncates rank, so none is unbiased and neither
reference is a bound on what we measure. We report them as reference curves,
with the constrained one primary because it is matched to the estimator's model
class, and the unconstrained one secondary because it describes the baseline
only.

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

Channels are drawn from the geometric model \eqref{eq:chan} with the parameters
of Section~\ref{sec:model}. Two generators are used. The first, written for this
work and denoted TB in Table~\ref{tab:config}, draws a fresh channel per trial
at a fixed SNR per operating point. The second, denoted TD, draws the SNR itself
per trial uniformly over $[-10,20]$~dB and bins results afterwards, which is why

sweeps needed many cells at many channel richnesses, and a per-trial draw covers
the range at lower cost. SNR and RSR are as defined in
Section~\ref{sec:model}, both measured at the array, and noise enters before the

three, and negative and significant at none. Abstention makes most trials
bit-identical to the baseline and pins the median at zero, while the active
minority dominates a sum of squared errors. The prediction is therefore
confirmed on the statistic a bound governs and is invisible to the one we
otherwise treat as primary. A reader taking only the median away would conclude
that the constraint is never harmful, and it is.

\label{sec:pathcount}

At configuration~D, holding $N=32$ and varying only the path count with $L$
% src: trackB_hankel_emgs/results/experiment_C_path_count.csv, configuration D
identical across users at $5$~dB SNR, the gain decays monotonically from
$+7.04$~dB at $L=2$ to $-0.12$~dB at $L=16=\rmax$, while the baseline moves by
$0.191$~dB in total. The operative variable is therefore channel complexity
relative to capacity, and aperture matters only because it sets that capacity.

\subsection{Effective rank, and the constraint versus the estimator}
\label{sec:effrank}

Path count remains imperfect as a coordinate, because clustered or
unequal-strength paths occupy fewer effective dimensions than their number
suggests. We index instead by the Roy--Vetterli effective
rank~\cite{royvetterli2007} of the lifting,
$\reff=\exp(-\sum_i p_i\ln p_i)$ with $p_i=\sigma_i/\sum_j\sigma_j$, normalised
by capacity as $\rho=\reff/\rmax$. That this is needed at all is easy to see: a
% src: reports/trackD_normalization.md:55, Track B Experiment C re-indexed;
% recomputed 8.694 at the same 200-trial budget, 8.669 at 1000 trials.
$16$-path channel on a $32$-element array is algebraically full rank yet has
effective rank $8.73$, so its lifting is compressible with nothing sparse about
it.

Re-indexing the path-count sweep at configuration~D moves the zero crossing from
$L/\rmax=0.90$ to $\rho=0.518$, and the useful region becomes
$\rho\lesssim0.5$ --- a statement about any channel rather than about one
simulator's $L$ values. In Fig.~\ref{fig:boundary}, $\dhs$ is plotted against
% src: reports/trackD_partB9_analysis.json B1_collapse; N=16,64 configuration C,
% N=32 configuration D. Crossings at N=16,64 are extrapolated, not bracketed.
$\rho$ for $N\in\{16,32,64\}$, with crossings $0.588$, $0.518$ and $0.544$, a
spread of $0.070$ over a fourfold change in aperture. This comparison spans two
configurations --- the $N=32$ value is configuration~D and the other two are
configuration~C --- and only the $N=32$ crossing is bracketed by measurements on
both sides. The other two are extrapolated from the positive side, because
abstention prevents the proposed estimator from producing negative values there.

That last point separates the constraint from the estimator, and the two must be
stated apart. Past the crossing at configuration~C the paired per-trial median
% src: results/p12/B2.json, 120-300 trials/cell, configuration C
never becomes negative: at $\rho=0.608$ and $0.735$ for $N=16$ it is $+0.078$
and $+0.000$~dB, and at $\rho=0.596$ and $0.669$ for $N=64$ it is $+0.044$ and
$+0.018$~dB. The reason is abstention --- at $\rho=0.735$ the selector returns
$\hat r\ge\rmax$ in $38.8\%$ of trials and the estimator is then exactly the
% src: results/p12/B2_forced.json -- DIAGNOSTIC ONLY, not the proposed method
baseline. Forcing the projection active at that same cell, as a diagnostic that
is not the proposed method, gives $-0.075$~dB with a confidence interval of
$[-0.186,-0.026]$. The constraint does become harmful past the crossing; the
abstention rule is what stops the typical trial from following it down.

This does not contradict the negative values reported above; the difference is
the statistic, by the mechanism just given. So $\dhs\ge0$ is a property of the
selector under a median statistic only, and is neither evidence that the
constraint is never harmful nor a guarantee about mean-square error. Where the
two statistics disagree, both are reported.

The magnitude of the gain does not collapse onto the same axis. Over the window
% src: reports/trackD_partB9_analysis.json PRIMARY_internal_N16_vs_N64, config C
both arrays cover at configuration~C, $N=16$ lies above $N=64$ by a mean of
$0.493$~dB and a maximum of $0.901$~dB, one-signed throughout, and we have no
account of this residual. The one candidate we tested --- that the selector's
% src: results/p12/B3.json, 250 trials/point, configuration C. The full pencil
% sweep (span 0.390 dB over rank counts 8 to 16, and the matched-shape pair
% 24x9 / 8x25 at +3.199 and +3.121 dB) is cut for space under PROMPT 18 Part F.
rank grid is coarser at small capacity --- is refuted by a pencil sweep at fixed
$N=32$ and fixed $\rho$, which shows no monotone trend with the number of
admissible ranks and tracks that count rather than the matrix shape.

Finally, $\rho$ is a design-time coordinate and not a receiver-observable one,
since $\reff$ is computed on the noiseless channel. The abstention rate is
observable and monotone in $\rho$ at fixed aperture, but confounded by capacity:
% src: reports/p12/a3_abstention_vs_rho.json, 16 adaptive-order cells
at $N=64$ a cell at $\rho=0.501$ abstains in $0.0\%$ of trials while at $N=16$ a
% (both cells configuration C)
cell at $\rho=0.371$ abstains in $4.0\%$, so no single threshold separates the
useful region. An observable proxy therefore exists within an aperture but has
not been established across apertures.

\subsection{Prediction on unseen channel distributions}
\label{sec:crossgen}

A relation fitted and evaluated on the same data proves little. We froze the
$\rho$-to-gain relation obtained above, committed numerical predictions to
version control, and only then ran two channel generators that had played no
part in constructing it. The rule is piecewise-linear interpolation on a fixed
eight-point table, with no regression and no spline, so it re-executes exactly.
Both tests use configuration~C's operator and operating point with the
generator replaced.

% src: reports/trackD_partB9_analysis.json, B6_xiao.B6_xiao_clustered
For a clustered Saleh--Valenzuela generator at $\rho=0.331$ the relation
predicted $+1.30$~dB, and the measurement over $276$ trials gave $+1.173$~dB
with a confidence interval of $[+1.017,+1.380]$, an error of $0.13$~dB. For a
% src: reports/trackD_partB9_analysis.json, B8_cui_measured
second, independently specified configuration at $\rho=0.400$ the prediction was
$+0.648$~dB with interval $[+0.367,+1.015]$, and the measurement over $266$
trials gave $+0.963$~dB with a confidence interval of $[+0.808,+1.086]$, an
error of $0.315$~dB --- inside the stated interval but on its upper edge.

These are cross-generator tests and not out-of-model tests. Both generators
still produce channels of the form \eqref{eq:chan}, and only the distribution
over $(L_k,\alpha_{\ell,k},\theta_{\ell,k})$ changes. They show that $\rho$
transfers across path distributions, not that it survives a violation of the
ULA model.

\subsection{Users, cost, and where the projection belongs}
\label{sec:kinv}

% src: reports/trackD_partB9_analysis.json, cells B2_K{2,3,4}, configuration C
At configuration~C and fixed pilot adequacy $P/2K=3.33$, the gain varies by
$0.094$~dB across $K\in\{2,3,4\}$ for SNR $\ge5$~dB, which is the invariance to

Order selection accounts for $57.7$--$85.5\%$ of runtime, so it, and not the
projection, is where an implementation should look for savings.

% src: results/p12/B5.json, 300 trials/point, shared \hat r, configuration A
Comparing interleaved projection against a single post-hoc projection at the
same selected order, at configuration~A, interleaving is worse at $0$~dB
($-0.164$~dB, interval $[-0.317,-0.066]$), indistinguishable from $+5$ to
$+15$~dB, and better only at $+20$~dB ($+0.168$~dB, interval
$[+0.102,+0.246]$), the six-point mean being $+0.003$~dB. Interleaving is
therefore not required in this classical iteration and we do not claim it is.

Since placement is a wash, what the estimator contributes is not \emph{where}
the projection is applied but \emph{whether} it is applied at all and at what
order, decided from held-out pilots and using no oracle. The evidence is the
abstention result above: the selector is what keeps $\dhs\ge0$, and forcing the
projection past the crossing costs $-0.075$~dB with interval
$[-0.186,-0.026]$. The order selector is the claim of this paper, not a
mechanism explained on the way to one.

\section{Limitations and Conclusion}

An amplitude-only receiver's information is rank one per measurement and block
diagonal across elements, so aperture is usable only through cross-element
structure. The exponential-sum structure of a ULA channel supplies it, with a
capacity of $\lceil N/2\rceil$; the constraint therefore helps only below a
fraction of that capacity, and the predicted sign reversal at small aperture is
observed. The governing coordinate is $\reff/\rmax$, whose boundary location is
stable across a fourfold change in aperture and which predicted the gain on two
unseen channel distributions to within $0.13$ and $0.315$~dB.

\textbf{Limitations.} All results are simulation only, with no measured
hardware and no recorded channel data. They cover geometric ULA channels of the
form \eqref{eq:chan} at the array sizes, pilot counts and generators tested, and
the cross-generator tests change the distribution but not the model family, so
no claim is made beyond them. The gain magnitude does not collapse onto the
proposed axis and its residual of $0.493$~dB is unexplained, and $\rho$ is not
receiver-observable. Two of the three boundary crossings cannot be bracketed
from the negative side, because abstention prevents it, and $\rho\approx0.75$ is
% src: rydberg_sim/channel.py:245 raises above L=48 at N=64
unreachable at $N=64$ in any case, where the path generator fails above $L=48$
($\rho\approx0.67$). We establish no fundamental bound: the references govern
unbiased estimators and every estimator here is biased.
