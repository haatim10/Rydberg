Two facts to read off this table, both of which are more important than the headline gain:

  • EM-GS is flat in N to 0.013 dB (−8.8456, −8.8484, −8.8360). This is not a coincidence or a well-behaved simulation; it is the row-separability of the exact-model estimators (§7) showing up in the data exactly as the theory says it must. It also serves as a strong internal consistency check: if the three N sweeps had a bug in the channel normalisation, this column would not be flat.
  • The constraint-active fraction tracks the gain. 57.5% → 92.7% → 99.6% against −0.19 → +0.78 → +2.85 dB. At N = 8 the projection is inactive in 43% of trials because L̂ hits the rank cap of 4, so at that array size HS-GS frequently is EM-GS.
The two poolings disagree in sign at N = 8 — both are correct

The mean gain column above is the unweighted mean of the twelve per-operating-point gains, i.e. "the average advantage at a typical operating point". At N = 8 it is −0.19 dB. If instead all 12 points' trials are pooled into a single ratio of sums, the same data gives +0.81 dB (and +1.04 dB restricted to constraint-active trials; median over active trials +0.21 dB). Neither number is wrong and neither is the "real" one.

The disagreement is the finding. Energy pooling weights each trial by its squared error, so it is dominated by the low-SNR points — where HS-GS wins by 0.78–1.47 dB. The unweighted mean gives the high-SNR points equal say — where HS-GS loses by up to 2.23 dB. At N = 8, HS-GS wins at low SNR and loses at high SNR (it is negative at 8 of the 12 points, all of them the higher-SNR ones), so any single scalar summary is a weighted vote between two opposite regimes. §16.6 identifies the mechanism: a bias-dominated error floor. Quoting either scalar alone, in either direction, would misrepresent the result; the per-point table in §16.1 is the honest report and is why it is printed in full.

The gap grows monotonically with N at every SNR. Restated: as the array grows, HS-GS recovers more of the structural gain in absolute terms (§16.2) while recovering a smaller share of what the structure makes available. Whatever is left on the table is growing, and §20 argues it lives in the estimator's two known weak points rather than in tuning.
