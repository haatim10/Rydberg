Before the receiver operation, the noiseless multi-user field is

Y = GS ∈ ℂN×P,     (GS)n,p = Σk=1K gn,k sk,p(6)

Meaning of one entry. (GS)n,p is the complex RF field that sensor n sees at pilot instant p: the coherent superposition of all K users' contributions. Each user's signal arrives with its own channel coefficient and its own pilot symbol, and they add as complex numbers — constructively or destructively. The receiver never observes the users separately; it observes one scalar field per sensor per instant, and then only its magnitude.

Part IV

Rydberg measurement physics

4.1 Atomic receiver interpretation

Scope boundary — read this before quoting anything in §4.1

The simulator does not model atomic physics. It contains no Rabi frequency, no energy levels, no Autler–Townes splitting, no optical probe, and no vapour cell. What it implements is the effective digital-domain consequence of the atomic readout: that the observation is the magnitude of a complex field, eq. (1). Everything in this subsection is physical justification drawn from the literature for why that effective model is the right one; none of it is simulated.

The physical chain, as described in the Rydberg atomic-receiver literature, runs: an incident RF electric field couples a pair of Rydberg states; the coupling strength is the Rabi frequency, which is proportional to the RF field amplitude; that coupling splits the optical transition (Autler–Townes), and the splitting is read out through the transmission of a probe laser. The observable at the end of the chain therefore tracks |ERF|. The optical readout is not phase-coherent with the RF carrier, so the RF phase does not survive to the photodetector.

4.2 The reference field — why it recovers phase

Superpose a known reference b on the unknown field x. The squared magnitude expands as

|x + b|² = |x|² + |b|² + 2 ℜ{x b*}(7)

The first term is the phase-blind intensity we would have measured anyway. The second is a known constant. The third is the payload: 2ℜ{x b*} = 2|x||b| cos(∠x − ∠b) depends on the phase of x relative to the known ∠b. So a magnitude measurement of the superposition encodes relative phase, even though a magnitude measurement of x alone would not. This is the same principle as holography and as a homodyne receiver.

4.4 Final measurement model

Z = |GS + B + W| ∈ ℝ₊N×P(8)

G, S, B and W are complex because they represent baseband field quantities carrying amplitude and phase. Z is real and non-negative because the sensor reports a field magnitude — the elementwise modulus, which maps ℂ → ℝ₊ and is where the information is lost. Verified: Z is float64, all entries ≥ 0, and matches |GS+B+W| to 0.00e+00.

Part V

SNR and RSR as implemented

Both definitions were re-derived from the code rather than assumed, because both contain a factor-K trap and the two use different denominators.

5.1 SNR

The implemented definition compares total received signal power against noise power per sensor:

SNR = E{|anHs|²} / E{|wn|²}(9)

With per-element channel power β = 1, unit-energy pilots and independent users, the numerator is Σ_k E|g_{n,k}|² E|s_k|² = K. Hence the noise variance actually set by the code is

σ² = K / SNRlin,   SNRlin = 10SNRdB/10(10)

Verified numerically: at a nominal 5 dB the code sets σ² = 0.94868 and K/SNR_lin = 0.94868 — identical. Measured achieved SNR on a generated world: 5.079 dB. Over 4 000 realisations with a bootstrap: 3.009 dB [2.957, 3.060] against a 3.00 dB target, i.e. unbiased.

Consequence worth stating: this is total power over all K users. Fixing SNR does not fix per-user SNR — doubling K at fixed SNR doubles σ².

5.2 RSR

RSR = E{|bn|²} / E{|an,ksk|²} ⟹ |αb| = √(RSRlin)(11)
The denominator is a single user, not the K-user sum

Measured on a generated world: RSR against the single-user denominator = 11.50 dB (nominal 12); against the total K-user power = 7.15 dB. The code implements the single-user convention (Cui eq. 37). Using the total would be wrong by 10log₁₀K = 4.77 dB, which is exactly the gap observed. The reference amplitude scaling was checked independently: |B| ratio between RSR = 24 dB and 0 dB is 15.85 against 1024/20 = 15.85.

5.3 Why RSR matters physically

  • Weak reference (RSR → 0 dB). The cross term 2ℜ{xb*} in eq. (7) is comparable to the phase-blind |x|² term. Phase information is present but poorly conditioned, and the observation is strongly nonlinear in G. This is where the problem is hardest and least like conventional channel estimation.
  • Useful interference (moderate RSR). The reference is strong enough to make the cross term dominant but not so strong that it swamps the signal. Measured: this is exactly where EM-GS's statistical weighting earns its advantage over plain GS — +1.3 dB at RSR = 0, decaying to +0.00 dB at RSR = 24 (§XIII, Figure A3).
  • Excessively strong reference (RSR ≫ 0). |b|² dominates, |x|² becomes negligible, and the observation becomes approximately linear in G. Statistically, the Bessel weighting saturates and EM-GS degenerates into plain GS.
  • Relation to the Xu approximation. The strong-reference limit is precisely the regime in which a linearised estimator becomes valid (§VIII). High RSR makes the problem easier but also makes the exact-model machinery unnecessary; low RSR is where exact methods should pay.
Part VI

The estimation problem

Given Z, S and B, recover G. The implemented objective corresponding to the exact model is

minimiseG   J(G) = ‖ Z − |GS + B| ‖F²(12)

Why the problem is nonlinear

The map G ↦ |GS+B| is a composition of a linear map with the elementwise modulus. The modulus is not linear, not holomorphic, and not even differentiable at zero. Consequently J is non-convex in G, has multiple stationary points, and admits no closed-form minimiser.

Why linear pilot inversion fails

If we observed Y = GS + B + W we would simply form (Y−B)SH(SSH)−1 and be done — a linear least squares with a unique solution. But we observe Z = |·|. The phase of every entry has been destroyed, so Y is not available and there is nothing to invert. Recovering Y from Z is itself the phase-retrieval problem. Inverting Z directly against S is meaningless: it treats a magnitude as if it were a complex field.

Why orthogonal pilots would not decouple the users

This point is worth making carefully, because it is a natural assumption and it is false here.

With orthogonal pilots and a linear observation, right-multiplying by SH separates users exactly, because SSH ∝ I. But orthogonality is a property of an inner product, and the magnitude operation destroys the linear structure that inner product acts on. Concretely, at instant p the sensor sees the scalar superposition Σ_k g_{n,k} s_{k,p}, takes its modulus, and discards the phase. Once that has happened, no linear post-processing can undo the sum: the users are entangled inside a nonlinear function, and |Σ_k …| ≠ Σ_k |…|. Orthogonality would help a linear receiver; it cannot help here.

Two supporting facts from the implementation: the pilots are in fact not orthogonal (§III, max off-diagonal 0.233), and the solvers never attempt user separation — they operate per receive element on all K users jointly (§VII).
