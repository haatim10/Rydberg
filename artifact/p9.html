Part XVII

Questions a professor will ask, and the answers

Twenty-four questions, chosen because each one is a place where this work could actually be wrong. Answers cite the numbers rather than asserting confidence. Where the honest answer is "this is a genuine weakness", it says so.

Q1 — the bound Your unconstrained CRLB is flat in N. More antennas must give more information. Isn't the bound simply wrong?

No, and the flatness is the strongest evidence that it is right. The metric is normalised MSE: E‖Ĝ−G‖²/E‖G‖². Adding a row adds P new measurements and 2K new unknowns, and — this is the crucial part — the Fisher information matrix is block diagonal across rows, because row n of GS enters only measurement row n. There is no coupling to share information across rows. So the per-row estimation problem is identical at every N, the total error grows exactly in proportion to the total energy, and the ratio is constant. The empirical check is decisive: EM-GS, an actual estimator, is flat to 0.012 dB across N ∈ {8,16,32}. A bound that moved while the estimator did not would be the broken one.

Q2 — the crossings Sixty-three of your 108 estimator/operating-point combinations sit below your CRLB, one by 6.94 dB. Why should I believe any bound in this document?

Because the CRLB bounds unbiased estimators, and none of these three is unbiased. The measured shrinkage coefficient c = ⟨Ĝ,G⟩/‖G‖² is 0.756 at SNR = −5 dB and 0.957 at SNR = +5 dB: the estimates are systematically pulled toward zero. A shrunk estimator trades bias for variance and can have strictly smaller MSE than any unbiased estimator — James–Stein is the textbook case. The prediction that follows is testable and it holds: crossings are largest exactly where c is smallest (low SNR, up to 2.6 dB for the baselines), and vanish where c → 1 (at SNR = 20 dB, P = 30 the baselines are 0.006–0.049 dB below the bound — numerically on it, within Monte Carlo error). HS-GS crosses by more because Cadzow's hard rank truncation is additional deliberate shrinkage. A bound that were merely miscomputed would not produce that ordered, SNR-dependent, estimator-dependent pattern. Separately: the originally shipped bound was wrong (Q20), was found, and is never plotted.

Q3 — units Why 10 log10 and not 20 log10? Half the channel-estimation literature uses 20.

Because the argument is already a power ratio. The numerator is ‖Ĝ−G‖²F and the denominator ‖G‖²F — both squared norms, i.e. energies. 20 log10 is for amplitude ratios, where the squaring has not yet been applied. Papers using 20 log10 are reporting ‖Ĝ−G‖/‖G‖ unsquared. Applying 20 log10 here would double every dB figure in this document, including the CRLB, so the comparison would remain internally consistent but every absolute number would be wrong by 2×. Verified in code: artifact_audit.json → db_factor.

Q4 — pooling Your N = 8 result is −0.19 dB under one pooling and +0.81 dB under another. That is a sign change. Are you choosing whichever supports your story?

The opposite: both are printed, in §16.2, with the explanation. The disagreement is not a defect of the analysis, it is the N = 8 finding. HS-GS at N = 8 wins at low SNR (+0.78 to +1.47 dB) and loses at high SNR (down to −2.23 dB). Energy pooling weights each trial by its squared error, so it is dominated by the low-SNR points and returns +0.81 dB. The unweighted per-operating-point mean gives each SNR equal say and returns −0.19 dB. Any scalar summary is a weighted vote between two opposite regimes, so no scalar should be quoted alone — which is precisely why all 36 points are tabulated in §16.1 rather than summarised. Note that N = 16 and N = 32 have no such ambiguity: every pooling agrees in sign, and the headline claim is the scaling in N, which is pooling-invariant.

Q5 — convergence HS-GS is an alternating map T = PS ∘ TGS where PS is a projection onto a non-convex set. What do you actually know about its convergence?

Very little, and the document says so rather than dressing it up. Specifically: (i) the fixed-rank Hankel variety is non-convex, so PS is not a projection onto a convex set and the standard alternating-projection convergence theory does not apply; (ii) Cadzow's inner loop is itself only an approximate projection — 4 sweeps of SVD truncation and anti-diagonal averaging, which is not the exact nearest point on the variety; (iii) TGS has no descent guarantee on the biased phase-retrieval objective either. There is no monotone merit function and no proof of convergence to a stationary point. What is known empirically: over all 28,000 Track-B trials at a fixed 50-iteration budget, no output was non-finite, and the iterate norm never diverged. That is a much weaker statement than convergence and should not be quoted as one. This is a real theoretical gap and it is listed in §20 as such.

Q6 — Kronecker's converse Kronecker's theorem gives rank = L only when the L exponentials are distinct. Your AoAs are continuous draws, so two can be arbitrarily close. What happens then?

Rank is a discontinuous function of the AoAs but the singular values are continuous. Two paths at ψ1 ≈ ψ2 give a Hankel matrix of algebraic rank L whose L-th singular value is small — it scales like the angular separation times the array aperture. So the exact rank statement survives (probability of exact coincidence is zero) but the numerical rank is L−1, and Cadzow truncating to L̂ = L−1 loses one path. This is not merely hypothetical: it is one mechanism by which the held-out selector picks L̂ below the true order, which it demonstrably does (§9.6: true (7,4,6), selected 5). Under a clustered channel this stops being an edge case and becomes the norm (§14.3), which is why the 38.901 experiment is the top-ranked next step.

Q7 — the shared order You impose a single scalar L̂ on all K users when the true orders were (7, 4, 6) and you selected 5. Every user's model is wrong. How is the method not simply invalid?

It is a real limitation and it is disclosed in §9.6 rather than buried. Three points in mitigation, none of which is a defence of the design: (i) the constraint applied is rank ≤ L̂, an inequality, so the user with Lk = 4 is not harmed — its Hankel matrix already has rank 4 ≤ 5 and passes through the projection nearly unchanged; (ii) the users with Lk = 7 and 6 are genuinely over-truncated, and that is exactly the shrinkage bias that shows up as high-SNR degradation (§16.6) and as CRLB crossings (Q2); (iii) the projection operates column-wise, so nothing couples the users except the shared choice of L̂ — making it per-user is a change to the selector, not to the estimator, and is the second-ranked next experiment in §20. The honest statement is: the reported gains are achieved despite a misspecified shared order, so they are a lower bound on what the structure can deliver, not an upper one.

Q8 — the handicap Order selection holds out 30% of the pilots. You never charged that cost to EM-GS. Isn't the comparison rigged in HS-GS's favour?

It is rigged in the opposite direction. EM-GS uses all P pilots for estimation. HS-GS uses all P for its final estimate too, but must first spend select_iter = 20 short inner runs on a 70/30 split to choose L̂ — extra computation and a noisier order estimate, with no extra data. There is no configuration in which HS-GS sees information EM-GS does not. The one thing HS-GS does get is the structural prior itself, which is the hypothesis under test. Had oracle Lk been used the comparison would have been unfair, which is why hs_gs_auto_uses_true_L was audited explicitly and is False; these are not ORACLE-RANK experiments.

Q9 — pilots Your pilots are i.i.d. Gaussian with off-diagonal |SSH|/P up to 0.233 — not orthogonal. Orthogonal pilots are standard and strictly better. Why?

Because the model here is not linear in the pilots. Under a linear observation Y = GS + W, orthogonal pilots decorrelate the users and are provably MMSE-optimal. Under Z = |GS + B + W| that argument does not go through: the modulus mixes users non-linearly, and pilot orthogonality no longer implies user separation at the estimator's input. Gaussian pilots are also what Cui uses, so the baselines are being run in their native regime — a requirement of reproducing rather than "improving" the reference. The generator does enforce P ≥ 2K and rejection-samples to guarantee full row rank, so the pilot matrix is never degenerate. Whether orthogonal pilots help under the modulus model is an open and interesting question; it is not one this study answers, and no claim about it is made.

Q10 — the operating point RSR = 12 dB is a very specific choice. How do I know it wasn't tuned until HS-GS won?

Three independent reasons, and the data settles it. (i) Provenance: 12 dB comes from Cui §VI-B's physical argument — an LO closer than a quarter of the user distance gives RSR > 10log10(4²) ≈ 12 dB — and is the value used throughout Cui's Figs. 5/7/8. It was inherited, not searched. (ii) It is the adversarial choice for the baselines, not for HS-GS. §16.7 shows the EM-GS-over-GS advantage collapsing from 1.285 dB at RSR = 0 to 0.002 dB at RSR = 24. At the 30 dB used in early scratch testing, R(κ) → 1 and Cui's two algorithms become numerically identical — the comparison would have been vacuous. 12 dB keeps them separable. (iii) The result does not depend on it. §16.7 sweeps RSR across five values at both N = 8 and N = 32: HS-GS loses at every RSR when N = 8 and wins at every RSR when N = 32, with the gain flat in RSR above 6 dB. The sign is set by N, not by RSR. Tuning RSR could not have produced this result.

Q11 — relevance of the non-linearity Cadzow denoising of a Hankel matrix is a 1980s technique. What does any of this have to do with the Rydberg modulus non-linearity? Isn't HS-GS just denoising bolted onto an unrelated problem?

The prior is indeed classical; the difficulty is where it is applied. The point is that the structural projection is interleaved inside the exact non-linear iteration — project_every = 1, so PS is applied after every EM-GS update, not once at the end as post-processing. That matters because the EM-GS update's quality depends on its current iterate through the Bessel-ratio phase estimate R(κ), κ = (2/σ²) z ⊙ |λ|: a structurally cleaner iterate produces a better phase estimate, which produces a better next iterate. The coupling runs both ways, which is exactly why the naive "denoise afterwards" framing does not describe what is implemented. Post-hoc denoising is a legitimate ablation and it is not the configuration reported. The novel content of this work is not Cadzow; it is (a) placing a hard structural projection inside a biased phase-retrieval iteration without linearising the measurement, and (b) the constrained CRLB that says how much such a thing could ever be worth.

Q12 — the shortfall The constrained CRLB says 9.85 dB of structural gain is available at N = 32, P = 10. You deliver 2.85 dB. Your method captures under a third of what it targets. Why present it?

Because the shortfall is a reported result, not a hidden one — §16.3 tabulates all 36 gaps and §13.3 plots them, showing the gap growing with N (+0.32 to +8.85 dB). Two things are worth separating. First, the CCRB is a bound for an estimator that knows the parameterisation and is unbiased; HS-GS knows only "rank ≤ L̂", uses a misspecified shared L̂ (Q7), and applies an approximate projection (Q5). It is not a parametric estimator and should not be expected to attain a parametric bound. Second, the growing gap is the most useful output of this analysis: it localises where the remaining gain lives — in per-user order selection and in replacing hard truncation with a genuinely parametric step — and it says the payoff for going there grows with array size. A study that reported only "+2.85 dB, good" would have thrown that away.

Q13 — cost HS-GS runs an SVD of an ⌈N/2⌉-square Hankel matrix at every one of 50 iterations, times 4 Cadzow sweeps, times K columns, plus 20 selection runs per candidate order. Is 2.85 dB worth that?

State the cost honestly first. Per iteration EM-GS costs O(NPK); the projection adds O(K · cadzow_iter · N³) — at N = 32 that is 4 SVDs of a 17×16 matrix per column per iteration. Measured against a chained EM-GS baseline (the correct comparison — HS-GS makes 50 separate max_iter=1 calls, which alone carries 1.77× call overhead) HS-GS matches EM-GS to within 0.5–3.8% at these dimensions, because N is small enough that the N³ term does not yet dominate NPK. Order selection is the real cost: it multiplies the whole estimation by the number of candidate orders. At larger N the N³ term would dominate and the answer would change. Whether 2.85 dB justifies it is an application question this document does not answer; the numbers to make that judgement are given.

Q14 — is EM-GS worth anything? At RSR = 12 dB, EM-GS beats plain GS by 0.117 dB. That is nothing. Why carry the EM machinery at all, and why build HS-GS on top of it?

The 0.117 dB is real and it is RSR-specific: at RSR = 0 dB the same comparison gives 1.285 dB (§16.7). The Bessel-ratio soft phase estimate matters precisely when the reference does not dominate — which is the physically interesting, low-reference regime. As RSR grows, R(κ) → 1, EM-GS's soft estimate becomes GS's hard modulus substitution, and the two provably coincide; the collapse to 0.002 dB at RSR = 24 is that limit being reached numerically. So the honest statement is that at the chosen operating point the choice of exact step inside HS-GS is nearly immaterial, and HS-GS's gain is attributable to the structural projection rather than to EM. That is a point against over-claiming for EM, and it is why exact_step is recorded as a hyperparameter (§11.3) rather than presented as a contribution.

Q15 — the redundancy ratio You define ρ(N) = 2NK/(3ΣLk) and use it to explain why larger arrays help. Is that an identifiability theorem?

No, and §9 says so explicitly. It is a parameter count: real measurements-worth of unknowns in the unstructured parameterisation, divided by real unknowns in the structural one. It is suggestive, not sufficient — a favourable count does not establish that the structural parameterisation is identifiable, that the map is injective, or that any algorithm attains it. The concrete counterexample is in this document: at N = 8, 42.5% of trials have 3ΣLk > 2NK, so the map φ → G is not injective and its Jacobian is rank-deficient (condition numbers to 6.8 × 1017). That is what forced the orthonormal tangent-basis construction in the constrained CRLB (Q20). ρ is used as intuition for why the gain should scale with N, and the scaling is then demonstrated empirically (§16.2) and tested directly against path count (§16.8); ρ is never the evidence.

Q16 — the angle prior Your AoAs are uniform in θ, not in sin θ. Which is physically correct, and does the choice change your conclusions?

Verified rather than assumed: a KS test over 4,465 samples gives p = 0.150 against uniform-in-θ and p = 0.0000 against uniform-in-sin θ, so the implementation is uniform in θ. Physically, uniform in sin θ (equivalently, uniform in electrical angle ψ) is what a spatially isotropic scatterer distribution produces in the far field; uniform in θ over-weights broadside. The choice matters for the constant in the CCRB, because the density of ψ affects how well-separated the Hankel modes typically are, but it does not touch the rank argument — Kronecker's theorem counts distinct exponentials regardless of their distribution — nor the N-scaling, which comes from the cap ⌈N/2⌉ growing while Lk does not. It is recorded as a verified model property, not defended as the better choice.

Q17 — the bootstrap You bootstrap a ratio of sums, which is a non-linear statistic, and report percentile intervals. Is that valid?

Reasonably, with a stated caveat. The ratio of sums is a smooth function of two sample means, so it is a standard smooth-function-of-means statistic for which the nonparametric bootstrap is consistent; trials are i.i.d. by construction, which is the assumption the bootstrap needs. The percentile interval (as opposed to BCa) can undercover when the statistic is skewed. Two things keep that from mattering here: the resample is paired across estimators, so for the gain statistic the shared channel realization cancels and the sampling distribution is close to symmetric; and the intervals are far from the decision boundary in the cases that carry the claim (at N = 32, P = 30 the gain CI is [2.12, 2.40] dB — nowhere near zero). Where intervals do straddle zero (several N = 8 and N = 16, P = 10 points) no claim is made from them. A BCa interval was not computed; that is a minor open item, not a load-bearing one.

Q18 — the failure mode Why does HS-GS lose at high SNR when N = 8? Structure should help more when there is less noise to hide behind.

It is a bias-dominated error floor, and the pattern is diagnostic. Total error decomposes into variance (falling with SNR) and squared bias (constant in SNR). Cadzow's truncation to L̂ < Lk removes real signal energy, contributing a bias term that does not shrink as noise falls. At low SNR variance dominates and the trade is worth it; as SNR rises, variance falls below the bias floor and HS-GS stops improving while EM-GS keeps going. The numbers follow that exactly: at N = 8, P = 10 the gain goes +1.47 → +0.85 → +0.10 → −0.78 → −1.32 → −2.23 dB monotonically as SNR goes −5 → 20 dB. The reason this is an N = 8 problem specifically is the rank cap: at N = 8 the cap is 4 while Lk ∈ {3,…,7}, so truncation is often severe and the bias floor is high. At N = 32 the cap is 16 ≫ Lk, truncation is mild, and the gain is roughly flat in SNR (+3.04 → +2.60 dB). Same mechanism, opposite regime.

Q19 — the linearisation constraint You insist nothing is linearised, but EM-GS replaces the modulus with a Bessel-ratio expression. Isn't R(κ) a linearisation in disguise?

No. A linearisation would replace z = |λ + w| with an approximate linear observation of G — e.g. z ≈ ℜ{λ e−j∠b} + const, valid for |b| ≫ |λ| — and then run linear least squares. That is what the Xu-style baseline in Part VIII does, which is why it is labelled LINEARIZED MODEL BASELINE and never used for any claim. R(κ) = I1(κ)/I0(κ) is something else: it is the exact conditional posterior mean of the phase factor under the true Rician likelihood, i.e. the exact E-step of EM for the exact model. No Taylor expansion is taken and no small-parameter regime is assumed. The audit checks this structurally: every trial asserts not r.linearised_model_used, and the data identity Z ≡ |GS+B+W| is verified to 0.0 (exact float equality) in artifact_audit.json.

Q20 — the constrained bound Why an orthonormal tangent basis? The textbook constrained CRLB is D(DTJD)−1DT. Why not just use that?

Because D is rank-deficient here, so (DTJD)−1 does not exist and pinv silently returns garbage. Concretely: at N = 8, 42.5% of trials have 3ΣLk > 2NK — more structural parameters than real channel coefficients — so the map φ → G is not injective and its Jacobian loses rank. The observed condition numbers reached 6.8 × 1017, i.e. numerically singular. The fix is Gorman–Hero / Stoica–Ng in its proper form: take U an orthonormal basis of range(D) via SVD with a relative tolerance of 10−9, and evaluate CCRB = U(UTJU)−1UT. This is invariant to over-parameterisation because it depends on the tangent space, not on the coordinates. Worst condition number after the fix: 80. The bound changed materially — at N = 8, P = 10, SNR = 5 dB it moved from −4.41 dB to −3.40 dB — so this was a correctness fix, not a cosmetic one.

Q21 — sample size 400 trials per point. How do you know that is enough?

From the intervals themselves rather than from a rule of thumb. The paired-bootstrap gain CI at the headline point (N = 32, P = 30, SNR = 5) is [2.12, 2.40] dB — a ±0.14 dB half-width on a 2.27 dB effect. Pairing is what makes that tight: the shared channel realization cancels, so the CI on the difference is far narrower than the marginal CIs. Where 400 was not enough the budget was extended rather than the claim weakened: five flagged points were run to 1,200 trials, all of them points where the gain CI straddled or nearly straddled zero (N = 8/16/32, P = 10, SNR = 5–20). Points whose conclusions are unambiguous were not extended, because more trials on an already-decided point buys nothing.

Q22 — falsifiability What result would have made you abandon this?

Three, stated as commitments rather than in hindsight. (i) If the gain did not scale with N — the entire mechanism claims the rank cap ⌈N/2⌉ growing relative to Lk is what creates the advantage, so a flat or non-monotone gain would kill it. Observed: −0.19 / +0.78 / +2.85 dB, monotone, with the N = 32 CI strictly above the N = 16 CI at 12/12 points. (ii) If the gain persisted when the constraint was vacuous — at L ≥ cap the projection provably does nothing, so a gain there would mean the improvement came from somewhere other than the claimed mechanism. This is what experiment B7 tests directly, with the hypothesis recorded in the script before the run (§16.8). (iii) If EM-GS were not flat in N — a moving baseline would mean the N-dependence was an artefact of normalisation. Observed flat to 0.012 dB.

Q23 — post-hoc experiments B7 was designed after you had seen B3's results. Isn't that a post-hoc test of a hypothesis you already knew the answer to?

Partly, and the distinction matters, so here is exactly what is and is not pre-registered. What was known from B3: the gain increases with N. What was not known and could not be read off B3: what happens as L increases at fixed N — B3–B6 all draw Lk ~ U{3..7} at random, so no controlled-L data existed at all. The prediction that the gain decays to zero at L = cap(N) = 16 is a quantitative, falsifiable consequence of the mechanism, and it was written into run_b7_pathcount.py's docstring before the run was launched — it is in the file's git history, not merely asserted here. That is as close to pre-registration as this setting allows. It is not a substitute for the model-class test (clustered channels, §14.4), which remains the experiment most able to falsify the work and has not been run.

Q24 — the two tracks Track A uses 3GPP TR 38.901 clustered channels; Track B uses an unclustered geometric model. Why should I trust conclusions from a codebase that models the same physics two different ways?

They answer different questions and the separation is deliberate. Track A reproduces Cui's published figures and therefore must use Cui's channel model; it is frozen at commit ce118f0 and nothing in Track B touches it. Track B asks whether ULA structure can be exploited, which requires a model in which "structure" is well defined — a sparse geometric model with an exact Hankel rank. Using 38.901 for Track B would have confounded the structural question with clustering effects from the outset. The cost of that choice is precisely the scope limit stated in §14.4: no Track-B result says anything about clustered channels. The right way to close the gap is to run B3 under the Track-A generator, which is the top-ranked next experiment in §20 — and it is ranked top because it is the one that could overturn the result, not because it would produce another figure.
